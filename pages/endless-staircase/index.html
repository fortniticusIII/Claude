The Endless Staircase

This sound keeps rising, forever, and never gets any higher. Put on headphones or turn the volume up a little, press Play, and listen for a minute. Then switch off the bell to hear the trick.

Silent. Press Play.

Try this: let it climb for twenty seconds with the bell on, then tap Bell on to turn it off. Without the bell, the top tone drops out of nowhere and a new bottom tone appears, and you can suddenly hear the loop restarting.

How it fools you

You are hearing about nine sine waves at once, each exactly one octave above the next. Octaves blend so well that your ear hears them as one note with a single pitch class, like "C" or "F sharp".

All nine tones rise together. The trick is the bell curve: tones in the middle are loud, tones at the edges fade to silence. By the time the highest tone gets too high, it is inaudible, and it quietly reappears at the bottom, also inaudible. Every moment you hear something going up, and you never hear anything come down.

Roger Shepard published the stepped version in 1964. Jean-Claude Risset made the smooth glide a few years later. Composers have used it since: the Shepard tone sits under a lot of tension-building film scores, and the same idea shows up visually in M. C. Escher's Ascending and Descending, the staircase monks climb forever.
