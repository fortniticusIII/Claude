Tokyo Slime Mold Rerun

In 2010, Tero and colleagues set oat flakes on a map of 36 places around Tokyo and let the slime mold Physarum polycephalum connect them. They reported a network about as cheap, efficient and fault tolerant as the region's railway, and a simple flow model that does the same. I rebuilt that model on a real coastline and terrain. I measured the actual JR network from Japan's national rail dataset on the same map, and set both against textbook ways of designing a network.

The model reproduces the paper's behaviour. On the paper's three measures, it beats the real JR network outright at several settings. So do two plain engineering heuristics. That says less about the slime than about the measures. They leave out what a railway is for. Against the heuristics, though, the slime model holds its own.

What came out

The model behaves as the paper says

More inflow (I₀) grows loops: cost and fault tolerance rise, routes shorten. A stronger feedback nonlinearity (γ) pushes the network toward a tree. At γ = 2.2 with low inflow it settles on a tree 0.6% shorter than the minimum spanning tree, by meeting at junctions between cities, as a Steiner tree does. Across three random seeds the cost varies by up to 8%, and the paper's own one-pair-per-step dynamics land within about 10% of my averaged version on every measure.

Measured honestly, JR scores worse than the paper's rail map

The paper put the rail network at 1.8 cost, 0.85 transport and 0.96 fault tolerance. Measured from the government track data on my map, the conventional JR lines that serve these places come to . Adding the Shinkansen raises fault tolerance to ; all heavy rail, private lines included, reaches at the cost. The paper's map was a selection of lines. Which selection you draw moves every number.

Several slime networks beat JR on all three at once

Five of the 28 masked runs are cheaper, shorter to travel and better protected than the JR lines. The closest in spirit, at γ = 1.2 and I₀ = 0.6, costs 1.54× the tree against JR's 2.25×, with routes 0.74 against 0.80 and fault tolerance 0.93 against 0.85.

So do textbook heuristics, which is the real lesson

A greedy spanner (add a direct link whenever the network detour exceeds 1.5× or 1.75×) also beats JR on all three, and so does the paper's own MST-plus-shortest-links family once it is dense enough. Beating a railway on length, average distance and single-break tolerance is easy. Those numbers do not see ridership, where people live, stations between the 36 points, gradients, speed, or a century of history. JR is built for those.

Against the heuristics, the slime model is strong

Below 3× the tree, 18 of 20 masked slime networks are beaten on all three measures by none of the 58 engineered networks. The exceptions are one degenerate tree and one high-γ run. The slime beats 11 engineered networks outright, mostly the MST-plus-links family. At equal cost it is far more robust: 0.93 fault tolerance against 0.71 at 1.54×.

The paper's single score rewards a ring

The paper summed up benefit per cost as α = FT ÷ cost. Its best simulation reached 0.7. The exact cheapest network that survives any single break, which I solved as an integer program, is essentially one loop through all 36 points: 1.26× the tree, α = 0.79, the highest of any network here. Routes on it are 72% longer than on the tree. Robustness per km means little without transport beside it, which is why the charts above keep all three. My bridge-covering heuristic adds, one at a time, the link that protects the most unprotected track per km. It reaches full fault tolerance at 1.51× with transport 0.85. That beats the paper's rail point on all three measures and comes close to its best simulation: α = 0.66 against 0.7 at the same transport.

How it was built

  • Map. A 1.5 km grid over the Kanto plain, 6,497 land nodes with links to their eight neighbours. Coastline from Natural Earth 10 m land. Lakes such as Kasumigaura come from bathymetry in the AWS terrain tiles (mean elevation below −1.5 m), since Natural Earth's lake layer has none here. Ground above 250 m is excluded. Runs without that mask came out nearly the same.
  • Food sources. The paper does not list its 36 points, so these are my reconstruction. Tokyo is seven hubs, as in the paper's model (Tokyo, Shinjuku, Ikebukuro, Shibuya, Shinagawa, Ueno, Kinshichō stations), plus 29 cities from Odawara to Narita and Kumagaya to Kisarazu. Coordinates are from Wikipedia.
  • Model. Flux through each tube follows the pressure drop, and each tube's conductivity relaxes toward a sigmoid of its flux:
Qij = Dij(pi − pj) / Lij  ·  dDij/dt = f(|Qij|) − Dij  ·  f(q) = qγ / (1 + qγ)
  • The paper picks one random source and sink per step. With a small step that averages to the mean of f over all 630 pairs, which one sparse factorisation per step gives exactly. I checked three settings against the stochastic version. I swept γ over 1.2, 1.5, 1.8 and 2.2, and I₀ from 0.3 to 16, with and without the high-ground mask: 56 runs in all.
  • Railway. MLIT National Land Numerical Information, railway sections N02 (2023). Lines drawn separately on a shared corridor are merged on a 100 m grid. Each food source snaps to its nearest station on the network being scored. Kasukabe and Tsukuba have no JR station; they snap 9 km and 10 km away for the JR variants. Spurs and loops that serve none of the 36 points are trimmed before scoring.
  • Fairness. Grid paths run 5% longer than straight lines on average (measured over 448 land pairs), so rail figures, measured on true track geometry, are scaled up 5% to match. Without that correction JR comes to 2.14× · 0.76 · 0.85, and four of the five slime runs still beat it on all three. Engineered networks link city to city along land routes. The slime model and the railway can also join at junctions between cities, which helps them.

Limits

  • The point set differs from the paper's, so its numbers and mine are comparable in kind, not to the decimal. I₀ depends on mesh spacing and does not transfer from the paper.
  • Fault tolerance here counts one break at a random point on the track. The paper counts by link, a number that depends on how junctions are drawn. Both are in the table.
  • Mesh spacing barely matters: on a 1.0 km grid, the γ 1.2, I₀ 1 network comes out at 1.76× · 0.71 · 0.99 against 1.75× · 0.73 · 0.98 at 1.5 km. Three seeds at that setting span 1.75–1.89× in cost.
