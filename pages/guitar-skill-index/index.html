51 guitarists · 8 measured dimensions · 240,000 simulated weightings

Who is the most skilled guitarist, by the numbers?

Guthrie Govan

How solid is that?

The win holds across every method tried, but it is narrower than the headline suggests. It comes from breadth: Govan is near the top on almost everything and weak on nothing. Once you add realistic measurement error, he is #1 in only about 38% of simulations, and a half-point downgrade across all eight scores would hand the top spot to Danny Gatton. Rank only the judged musicianship scores (harmony, polyphony, improvisation, touch, precision) and Tuck Andress comes first. Govan is the most defensible single answer. He is not a lock.

Step 1 · What gets measured

Eight dimensions of playing skill

Only abilities you can in principle measure from a recording or a transcription count. Fame, sales, influence, songwriting and "feel" as public opinion are all excluded. Three dimensions are raw counts; five are 0–10 scores estimated from recorded evidence.

Step 2 · The math

Removing the opinion from the weights

Any ranking needs weights: is speed worth more than harmony? Choosing them myself would sneak opinion back in. So the analysis never picks one set. It checks who wins across all of them.

  1. Put every dimension on one scale

    Each dimension is rescaled 0–1 across the pool (min–max), so notes per second and genre counts can be combined.

  2. Find the Pareto front

    Drop anyone who is matched or beaten on all eight dimensions by someone else. of 51 survive. Only these players can be #1 under any weighting at all.

  3. Try every possible weighting

    200,000 weight vectors drawn uniformly from the whole space of weightings (a flat Dirichlet on the 8-simplex). Each draw produces a full ranking; I record how often each player finishes #1 and their average rank.

  4. Add measurement error

    40,000 more draws with random noise on every score (±1.5 notes/sec, ±0.75 points, ±1 genre, ±1 technique, one standard deviation), because my estimates are not lab measurements.

  5. Let the data pick the weights

    Three standard "objective weighting" methods from decision science set weights from the data itself: Entropy (EWM), CRITIC and the first principal component. Each is combined three ways (weighted sum, geometric mean, TOPSIS), plus a Borda count: 13 methods in total.

  6. Run it as an election

    For every pair of players, whoever scores higher in most weightings wins that matchup. A player who wins every matchup is a Condorcet winner.

        What this can't tell you

        Limits of the model

        • The inputs are estimates. Nobody has onset-analyzed and transcribed these 51 catalogs on one standard. My scores come from recorded performances and documented work. The noise test exists to show how much that matters, and it matters a lot.
        • The checklists reward breadth. Genres and techniques come from fixed lists of 15 and 17 items. Splitting classical or flamenco into finer sub-skills would help the specialists. Versatility is a real skill, but how you count it is a choice.
        • Output only, not constraints. Django Reinhardt played with two working fretting fingers, and Segovia and Django are judged from old recordings. This index measures what reached the recording, not what it took to get there.
        • The pool decides who can win. I picked 51 players across rock, metal, blues, jazz, classical, flamenco, country and bluegrass. Anyone left out cannot win.
        • Speed has diminishing returns. The Guinness record for guitar speed is held by players who rank nowhere here. That is why no single dimension can decide this.
