Why jazz sweeps the top nine

Averaging individual skill rewards bands where every seat is a virtuoso who improvises over hard harmony. That describes jazz and fusion groups almost by definition. Most rock bands have one or two elite players and a steady supporting seat, which pulls the average down. The index measures the players in a lineup. It does not measure songs, sound, or how well the band played together.

Step 1 · Scoring a musician

One scale for every instrument

You can't compare a drummer's notes per second with a singer's. So each seat is scored 0–10 against the ceiling of its own instrument: 10 means among the best ever on that instrument, on that dimension. That lets a bassist, a saxophonist and a singer sit on one scale.

A seat is one member's main instrument. A principal lead singer gets a second seat for the voice, so Geddy Lee counts as Rush's bassist and as Rush's singer. Band score = the average of its seats.

Step 2 · The math

Same approach as the guitarist index

  1. Score every seat

    Each of 199 seats gets seven 0–10 scores from recorded evidence, each relative to its instrument's ceiling. The band's classic lineup is used.

  2. Average the seats

    A seat's score is the weighted mean of its seven dimensions. The band's score is the plain average of its seats.

  3. Try every weighting

    100,000 weight vectors drawn uniformly across all ways of balancing the seven dimensions. I record how often each band is #1 and its average rank.

  4. Add measurement error

    20,000 more draws, each adding independent noise (±0.75 points, one standard deviation) to every score of every seat.

  5. Swap the averaging rule

    Five other ways to combine members: average per person instead of per seat, each person's best role only, the median seat, the weakest seat, and the geometric mean.

    What this can't tell you

    Limits of the model

    • The inputs are estimates. Nobody has measured these 199 seats on one standard. Scores come from recordings and documented work. The noise test shows how much that uncertainty moves things; the top two stay stable, and the rock race shifts.
    • No ensemble score. You asked for individual skill averaged across members, so tightness, arrangement, songwriting and chemistry are left out. A band could rank low here and still be the better band.
    • "Relative to the instrument's ceiling" is a judgment. It decides how a great drummer compares with a great singer. There's no neutral way to do that; this rule at least treats every instrument the same.
    • Lineup choice matters. Each band is scored at one classic lineup. Dream Theater with Mike Mangini or Yes with Trevor Rabin would score differently.
    • The pool decides who can win. 44 bands across jazz, fusion, rock, prog, metal and alt. Funk, soul and big bands (Tower of Power, Earth, Wind & Fire) aren't in it yet.
    Method: per-instrument 0–10 seat scores, seat-mean aggregation, Dirichlet Monte Carlo over 7 dimension weights (seed 7), Gaussian seat-level noise, five alternative aggregators. Companion to The Guitar Skill Index.
